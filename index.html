About This Visualization

This visualization explores how wastewater surveillance detected COVID-19 outbreaks before hospitals saw the surge. Using data from the CDC's National Wastewater Surveillance System (NWSS) and clinical case reports, it reveals the lead time between underground detection and surface-level outbreaks.

The Metaphor

Inspired by Stranger Things, the visualization frames wastewater data as the Upside Down, a hidden layer of reality where threats are detectable before they break through to the surface. This is not just decoration. Byrne et al. (2019) showed that figurative frames in visualization expand expressiveness by layering descriptive meaning onto abstract data.

How to Read It

The Upside Down (red layer): Glowing red dots show wastewater sampling sites detecting viral RNA. Larger, brighter dots indicate stronger signal at higher-capacity treatment plants.

The Breach (3D tilt): When clinical cases surge, the scene tilts to 65° revealing two transparent map layers stacked in Z-space. The blue surface layer returns and lands on top of the red layer. Amber cracks then grow where the virus broke through to hospitals, with crack intensity reflecting how strong the wastewater signal was at each site.

Ridgeline plot: Shows wastewater signal (red filled) vs clinical cases (blue dashed) per region. The horizontal gap between peaks is the lead time.

Hover a region to compare it: Moving your cursor over any row brings that region forward in full colour while the others dim back. This is the easiest way to see how far ahead the red wastewater curve sits from the blue clinical curve. Where the two curves sit almost on top of each other, that region had no early warning at all.

Lead times (⚡ button): Reveals the measured lead time for every state in the current wave, computed by cross-correlating weekly wastewater levels against weekly case counts.

Two Ways to Measure a Lead

The numbers in the lead time panel come from cross-correlation, which slides the entire wastewater curve forward and asks which shift best matches the whole clinical curve across every week in the wave. The gap you can see on the ridgeline is something else, the distance between two single points, the highest wastewater week and the highest clinical week.

On a clean curve with one peak the two agree. On a noisy or double-peaked curve they do not, which is why one region can show a two week correlation lag while its peaks sit in the same week. Only one region per wave is annotated with a span, chosen because it is the region where both measures agree, so the annotation never contradicts what your eye reads. The other rows are left clean for comparison.

A hard limit: the case data is weekly, so no lead shorter than seven days can be measured here at all. Published studies put most lead times between two and eight days, which is at or below that floor. A region marked under 1 week has not been shown to lack a lead. It has been shown to have one too short for weekly data to resolve.

Coverage and Unstable Signals

Sampling is very uneven. Illinois has 71 treatment plants covering 62% of the state. Idaho has one covering under 3%. Sites in thinly covered states are drawn dim and flickering rather than solid, so a barely monitored place cannot pass for a well monitored one. Hover any dot to see its state's coverage.
