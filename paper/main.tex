\title{Structure Before Style. Choosing a Science Fiction Frame\\ That Matches the Data}

\author{Jacie Jermier\thanks{jjermier@uwaterloo.ca}\\ %
        \scriptsize University of Waterloo}

\abstract{%
Wastewater surveillance can detect SARS-CoV-2 outbreaks days to weeks before
clinical case counts rise, yet existing public-facing dashboards communicate this
lead time through static line charts and choropleth maps. We present \emph{The
Upside Down}, a narrative visualization that uses a \emph{Stranger Things}
inspired figurative frame to make the lead-lag relationship between wastewater
signals and clinical cases intuitively accessible. We argue that a science
fiction frame should be chosen for how well its structure maps onto the data
relationship, not for whether the theme feels relevant. Testing our own frame
against computed lead times forced us to drop our most dramatic variant wave and
to show a region where the early warning story is false.%
}

\keywords{Narrative visualization, figurative frames, science fiction,
wastewater surveillance, early warning.}

\section{Position}

Infected individuals shed SARS-CoV-2 RNA into wastewater before symptoms appear
and before they seek testing~\cite{daoust2021,peccia2020}. Public health
authorities can therefore detect outbreaks days to weeks earlier than
hospital-based reporting allows~\cite{kumar2022}.

Existing visualizations of wastewater surveillance have remained limited to
time-series overlays and choropleth maps. These representations are analytically
useful for experts but fail to show what makes this data so compelling. The virus
was detectable underground days to weeks before it ever showed up in hospitals.

The \emph{Stranger Things} metaphor fits here because viewers already understand
the show's central concept of a parallel dimension occupying the same physical
space.

Our position is that figurative frames in visualization are not merely aesthetic
choices but structural decisions that shape how viewers interpret data
relationships~\cite{byrne2019}. The Upside Down metaphor does not just decorate
the lead-lag relationship. It makes that relationship spatial by placing the
virus below in wastewater before it appears above in hospitals. Choosing a
metaphor should be driven by how well the metaphor's structure maps onto the data
relationship, not just whether the theme feels relevant.

That claim gives us a way to test a frame. If the metaphor really does encode the
relationship, it should visibly break wherever the data stops holding.

\section{System}

The core visual metaphor uses two transparent deck.gl map layers rendered inside
a shared 3D scene. The bottom layer, the Upside Down, renders U.S. state outlines
in red, overlaid with wastewater detection dots whose radius and brightness
encode viral RNA percentile intensity and population served
(Fig.~\ref{fig:detection}). The top layer, the surface, renders identical
outlines in blue, overlaid with procedurally generated crack paths whose severity
encodes clinical case intensity weighted by the wastewater signal at each site.
This means the places where wastewater detected the most virus are the places
that crack hardest when clinical cases arrive. When the scene is flat the two
layers overlap perfectly. When tilted to 65 degrees they separate in Z-space,
revealing the void between the two worlds (Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}).

A D3.js ridgeline plot stacks regional rows. Each row contains a filled
wastewater curve in red and a dashed clinical curve in blue, and the horizontal
displacement between their peaks directly encodes the lead time for that region
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ridge}).

Our design follows Segel and Heer's martini glass structure~\cite{segel2010}. The
lead-lag relationship only makes sense if the viewer first sees the wastewater
phase before the clinical phase.

\section{Testing the Frame}

Lead time values in our first version were conservative manual estimates rather
than statistically computed values. We replaced them with a cross-correlation of
weekly wastewater percentile against weekly clinical cases. Two things came out
of that.

The first is that the Delta wave had to be removed. CDC NWSS percentile reporting
does not begin until 15 November 2021, so the entire Delta window contains no
viral intensity data. Delta had been our flagship wave and it carried most of our
figures. The visualization had been rendering it confidently the whole time.
Nothing in the design forced the metaphor to break, which is the exact failure we
are warning about.

The second is that wastewater surveillance does not produce a clean and uniform
lead time across every state and region (Table~\ref{tab:leads}). Idaho led
Omicron by three weeks and Arizona led BA.5 by two. West Virginia led neither.
Its correlation peaks at lag 0 in both waves, which means the wastewater signal
and the clinical signal move together. There is no underground.

This unevenness is not a flaw in the visualization but a reflection of the
real-world variability documented by Kumar et al.~\cite{kumar2022}. We now show
Appalachia and West Virginia rather than leaving them out
(Fig.~\ref{fig:counter}). In those rows the two curves sit on top of each other
and the layers have nothing to separate. The frame fails in public, which is the
strongest evidence we have that it was encoding something real.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{Pick frames by structural correspondence rather than thematic fit.} Many
stories are epidemiologically evocative. Almost none of them place a hidden layer
beneath a visible one in the same physical space, and that property is what does
the work here.

\textbf{A speculative frame should be able to fail.} Our metaphor's credibility
rests on the regions where it does not hold. A speculative interface that cannot
fail against real data is closer to concept art, which is valuable but a
different contribution.

\textbf{A fiction carries its source's blind spots.} The Upside Down implies that
the underground always knows first. Before late 2021 there was no underground to
look at because the sensors had not been installed yet. A frame that cannot
express the absence of instrumentation will quietly imply coverage that never
existed.

\section{Limitations}

Lead times are computed from aggregated weekly series, and sewer transit time and
sampling frequency are not modeled~\cite{kumar2022}. Temporal coverage spans
Omicron and BA.5 only. The 3D tilt effect requires a modern browser with
hardware-accelerated CSS transforms and WebGL support, which excludes older
devices and some mobile browsers. We report correlations rather than causal lead
estimates.
